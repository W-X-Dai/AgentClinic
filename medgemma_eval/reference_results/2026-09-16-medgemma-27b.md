Across all 120 paired cases, images changed three wrong answers to correct and five
correct answers to wrong (delta −1.7 percentage points; exact McNemar p=0.727). In
the 90 direct-diagnosis cases, images produced one helpful and four harmful flips
(delta −3.3 points; exact McNemar p=0.375). Neither comparison supports a reliable
overall image benefit in this run.

## What this run suggests

For direct-diagnosis questions, the strongest adequately sized multimodal subtypes
were `hist/path` (90.0%, n=10) and `dermatology` (84.6%, n=13). The weakest
adequately sized groups were `physical` (68.3%, n=41) and `CT scan` (68.8%, n=16).
These are descriptive rankings, not statistically established differences.

The clearest potentially useful visual signal was in the full `xray` group, where
two cases improved and none worsened. Conversely, adding images slightly reduced
overall accuracy and produced harmful flips in `physical` and `ophthalmology`
questions. The correct conclusion is not that MedGemma is categorically bad at
those images; it is that this run did not demonstrate a general multimodal gain and
identified those groups for closer case-level error review.

## Limitations

- Many question stems already describe the image or pathology in text. Equal
  text-only and multimodal scores therefore do not prove that the image was ignored,
  and correct multimodal answers do not prove visual grounding.
- The 120 public NEJM cases may overlap model pretraining data.
- This is a five-choice benchmark. It does not measure open-ended diagnostic
  equivalence, calibration, treatment safety, or performance in clinical workflow.
- Several subtypes have too few cases for useful inference. One option permutation
  and one deterministic seed were used; option-order robustness needs repeated,
  predeclared seeds.
- A mismatched-image negative control and clinician-reviewed case-level error audit
  would be needed before making stronger claims about visual reliance.
